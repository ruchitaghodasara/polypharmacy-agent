WARFARIN AND ANTIPLATELET AGENTS
Concurrent use of warfarin with antiplatelet drugs such as aspirin, clopidogrel, or NSAIDs substantially increases the risk of major bleeding events including gastrointestinal haemorrhage and intracranial bleeding. Aspirin inhibits platelet cyclooxygenase and also displaces warfarin from plasma protein binding sites, elevating free warfarin concentrations and prolonging INR beyond therapeutic targets. Clinical studies indicate that combination therapy raises absolute annual bleeding risk by 1–3% compared to warfarin monotherapy. Monitoring: check INR within 3–5 days of initiating any antiplatelet agent. Consider proton pump inhibitor co-prescription for gastric protection. Alternative: if antiplatelet therapy is required alongside anticoagulation, prefer clopidogrel at the lowest effective dose under specialist supervision.

WARFARIN AND STATINS (CYP2C9 / CYP3A4 INTERACTIONS)
Several statins inhibit cytochrome P450 enzymes responsible for warfarin metabolism. Atorvastatin and simvastatin are metabolised by CYP3A4 and can modestly inhibit warfarin clearance, raising INR by 10–30%. Fluvastatin and rosuvastatin inhibit CYP2C9 and carry a stronger interaction potential. Patients stabilised on warfarin who are newly started on a statin should have INR checked at 5–7 days. Dose reductions of warfarin by 10–15% may be needed. Pravastatin and pitavastatin have minimal CYP interactions and are preferred when initiating statin therapy in anticoagulated patients.

METHOTREXATE TOXICITY AND NSAIDs
Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) including ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, and aspirin reduce the renal tubular secretion of methotrexate by competing at the organic anion transporter. This mechanism causes methotrexate plasma concentrations to rise to toxic levels even at standard weekly rheumatological doses of 7.5–25 mg. Toxicity presents as mucositis, bone marrow suppression (leucopenia, thrombocytopenia), hepatic fibrosis, and nephrotoxicity. Mortality has been reported. Interaction risk is highest in patients with pre-existing renal impairment or hypoalbuminaemia. Management: avoid all NSAIDs in patients on weekly methotrexate. Substitute paracetamol for analgesia. If NSAID is unavoidable, withhold it 24 hours before and 48 hours after the methotrexate dose and monitor full blood count and creatinine weekly.

METHOTREXATE AND FOLIC ACID SUPPLEMENTATION
Folic acid (5 mg weekly, taken on a non-methotrexate day) is routinely co-prescribed with methotrexate in rheumatoid arthritis to reduce folate-depletion side effects including mucositis, elevated liver enzymes, and gastrointestinal intolerance. Folic acid supplementation does not reduce the disease-modifying efficacy of methotrexate. It is a therapeutic adjunct, not a drug interaction. Folinic acid (leucovorin) is used in oncological methotrexate rescue protocols where much higher doses are employed. Patients should be counselled not to take folic acid on the same day as methotrexate, as it may competitively reduce methotrexate uptake at the cellular level.

METHOTREXATE AND TRIMETHOPRIM
Trimethoprim (including co-trimoxazole/Bactrim) is a dihydrofolate reductase inhibitor. When co-administered with methotrexate, both drugs deplete the same folate pathway, producing profound and potentially fatal bone marrow suppression. This combination is considered absolutely contraindicated. Cases of severe pancytopenia, agranulocytosis, and death have been reported even with a single dose of trimethoprim in patients on maintenance methotrexate. Alternative antibiotics for common infections in patients on methotrexate: amoxicillin, cefalexin, or nitrofurantoin (for UTIs), avoiding all sulfonamide-trimethoprim combinations entirely.

QT PROLONGATION — HYDROXYCHLOROQUINE AND PSYCHIATRIC DRUGS
Hydroxychloroquine prolongs the cardiac QT interval through blockade of hERG potassium channels (IKr current). Many psychiatric medications share this mechanism, including SSRIs (particularly citalopram and escitalopram at high doses), tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline, imipramine), antipsychotics (haloperidol, quetiapine, ziprasidone), and lithium. Sertraline carries a lower but non-negligible QT risk. Concurrent use of hydroxychloroquine with any QT-prolonging psychiatric agent requires a baseline 12-lead ECG. The QTc interval should be rechecked at 4–6 weeks and whenever the dose of either agent is changed. A QTc exceeding 500 ms is a hard stop — discontinue the offending agent and obtain cardiology review.

QT PROLONGATION — AMIODARONE
Amiodarone is a class III antiarrhythmic that substantially prolongs QT interval as its primary mechanism of action. Combining amiodarone with other QT-prolonging agents including hydroxychloroquine, fluoroquinolone antibiotics, macrolide antibiotics, methadone, or antifungals (fluconazole, ketoconazole) creates a high risk of Torsades de Pointes and potentially fatal ventricular fibrillation. This combination is generally contraindicated. ECG monitoring alone is insufficient protection — pharmacological synergy at the ion channel level means the combined effect exceeds what QTc measurement predicts.

ACE INHIBITORS AND NSAIDs — RENAL AND BLOOD PRESSURE EFFECTS
ACE inhibitors (lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril, perindopril) reduce angiotensin II-mediated vasoconstriction of the renal efferent arteriole, lowering intraglomerular pressure and providing nephroprotection in diabetic nephropathy. NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandin synthesis, causing afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction and reducing glomerular filtration. When combined with an ACE inhibitor, this creates a "double hit" on renal perfusion that can precipitate acute kidney injury (AKI), particularly in elderly patients, those with volume depletion, or pre-existing chronic kidney disease. Additionally, NSAIDs cause sodium and water retention that directly antagonises the antihypertensive mechanism of ACE inhibitors, raising blood pressure by 3–5 mmHg on average. In patients with heart failure on ACE inhibitors and diuretics, NSAID addition can trigger acute decompensation. Management: avoid NSAIDs where possible. If short-term use is unavoidable, monitor serum creatinine, potassium, and blood pressure within 2 weeks.

ACE INHIBITORS AND POTASSIUM-SPARING DIURETICS
ACE inhibitors block aldosterone, reducing urinary potassium excretion. Potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, eplerenone, amiloride) act via the same downstream pathway. Combination use causes hyperkalaemia — serum potassium may rise to 6.0–7.0 mEq/L, at which levels cardiac arrhythmias (bradycardia, AV block, ventricular fibrillation) are at high risk. In heart failure the combination is sometimes clinically necessary (with careful monitoring), but outside this indication it should generally be avoided. Potassium supplementation, salt substitutes containing KCl, and NSAIDs all further raise the risk. Monitoring protocol: check serum potassium and creatinine at 1 week after initiating combination, then monthly for 3 months, then quarterly.

CORTICOSTEROIDS AND NSAIDs — GASTROINTESTINAL RISK
Corticosteroids (prednisone, prednisolone, dexamethasone) impair gastric mucosal defence by suppressing prostaglandin-mediated mucus and bicarbonate secretion, thinning the protective gel layer of the stomach. NSAIDs independently damage the gastric mucosa through the same prostaglandin pathway. Concurrent use multiplies the risk of peptic ulcer disease and gastrointestinal perforation 15-fold compared to NSAID use alone. All patients receiving both a corticosteroid and an NSAID (even short-term) should be co-prescribed a proton pump inhibitor. Misoprostol is an alternative when PPI is not tolerated.

CORTICOSTEROIDS AND DIABETES MANAGEMENT
Systemic corticosteroids including prednisone, prednisolone, and dexamethasone cause dose-dependent insulin resistance and stimulate hepatic gluconeogenesis, producing steroid-induced hyperglycaemia. In patients with type 2 diabetes this typically worsens control significantly — fasting glucose may rise 2–4 mmol/L and post-prandial glucose spikes further. Short-acting corticosteroids cause predominantly post-prandial hyperglycaemia; long-acting preparations (dexamethasone) affect fasting glucose more uniformly. Management: monitor fasting blood glucose daily during corticosteroid courses. Dose-adjust existing oral hypoglycaemics upward; consider adding a sulfonylurea or NPH insulin if required. Metformin dose adjustment is often insufficient — consult diabetologist for regimens exceeding 1 week.

HYDROXYCHLOROQUINE AND HYPOGLYCAEMIA
Hydroxychloroquine has a clinically significant glucose-lowering effect in patients with type 2 diabetes, estimated at 0.5–1.0% reduction in HbA1c when used for autoimmune indications. The mechanism involves enhanced insulin receptor sensitivity and reduced lysosomal degradation of insulin. When hydroxychloroquine is added to existing antidiabetic therapy — particularly metformin, sulfonylureas, or insulin — additive hypoglycaemia risk increases. Patients should be warned about hypoglycaemia symptoms (diaphoresis, tremor, palpitations, confusion). Fasting blood glucose should be checked at 2 and 6 weeks after initiating hydroxychloroquine in any diabetic patient.

SSRI ANTIDEPRESSANTS AND ANTIPLATELET / BLEEDING RISK
Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) including fluoxetine, sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram, and paroxetine deplete intraplatelet serotonin by blocking the serotonin transporter (SERT) on platelet membranes. Serotonin normally amplifies platelet aggregation via the 5-HT2A receptor. SSRI-treated patients therefore have functionally impaired platelet aggregation. When combined with other antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs (aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin, heparin), the bleeding risk is amplified substantially. Observational studies show a 3–4-fold increase in upper gastrointestinal bleeding when SSRIs are combined with NSAIDs. A proton pump inhibitor should be co-prescribed in any patient receiving an SSRI with aspirin or an NSAID.

AMLODIPINE AND SIMVASTATIN — CYP3A4 INHIBITION
Amlodipine is a moderate inhibitor of CYP3A4. Simvastatin is metabolised almost exclusively by CYP3A4, making it highly susceptible to CYP3A4 inhibition, which leads to elevated simvastatin plasma concentrations and increased risk of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis. The FDA recommends limiting simvastatin to 20 mg daily when co-prescribed with amlodipine. Symptoms of statin myopathy include proximal muscle weakness, myalgia, and elevated creatine kinase. Rhabdomyolysis, though rare, can cause acute renal failure. Preferred alternative: switch to atorvastatin or rosuvastatin, which are not subject to the same degree of CYP3A4-mediated accumulation in the presence of amlodipine.

LEVOTHYROXINE ABSORPTION INTERACTIONS
Levothyroxine has a narrow therapeutic index and its absorption from the gastrointestinal tract is highly sensitive to co-administered agents. Calcium carbonate, iron supplements, antacids (aluminium hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide), cholestyramine, and proton pump inhibitors all reduce levothyroxine bioavailability by 20–40% when taken concurrently. Levothyroxine should be taken on an empty stomach, 30–60 minutes before food and at least 4 hours apart from any of the above agents. Coffee also impairs absorption. Patients switching from fasting administration to fed administration may develop clinical hypothyroidism even without any dose change.

DRUG INTERACTIONS IN RENAL IMPAIRMENT
Renal impairment alters the pharmacokinetics of many renally-cleared drugs, increasing the risk of toxicity from accumulation. Metformin is renally cleared and must be dose-reduced or stopped at eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m2 due to lactic acidosis risk. Methotrexate is also primarily renally excreted — even mild renal impairment significantly prolongs methotrexate half-life and raises toxicity risk. Hydroxychloroquine can accumulate in severe renal failure and requires monitoring. NSAIDs should generally be avoided in patients with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73m2, particularly in combination with ACE inhibitors or ARBs, as the risk of AKI is substantially higher.
